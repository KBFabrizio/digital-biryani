It's early September, and the former Halyard frontman is playing one of his first solo shows on this side of the Atlantic, in a low-ceilinged room in Bushwick that holds maybe three hundred people. Rourke has come back from a long break (which, by his own account, involved a lot of late nights and very little songwriting) after his old band split in 2015. He and a four-piece backing band tear through a run of Halyard favorites, along with songs from his forthcoming solo record, Low Tide Radio: the jittery, shout-along “Glasshouse” and the slower, more reflective “Harbour Street.”

Then he starts “Northern Line,” and the crowd loses its mind, but he stops the song cold two bars before the big singalong finish. He puts the guitar down and walks off. A stranger at the bar tells me afterward that it was the best thing he's seen all year, and that stopping a hit halfway through is exactly what Rourke would do. I checked, and the stranger is right. Six weeks later, Rourke calls me from a hotel in Rotterdam after a show and spends an hour proving he's still the most quotable person in guitar music. We talk about the long break, the slow thaw with his old bandmate, the twentieth anniversary of the Halyard album that nearly broke them, a hotel clerk in Glasgow who wouldn't give him a room key without ID, and the new record, which turns out to be gentler than anyone expected.

Rourke's mood shows in the way he moves more clearly than in anyone else I've interviewed. When he's restless, he paces the length of a room. When he's content, he goes very still and watches people. Either way, the people around him notice within a minute, whether they're in a rehearsal studio, a car, a dressing room or an arena.

At 44, Rourke hasn't lost the cheerful swagger that drove his bandmate out of Halyard in 2014. He tells me he likes to talk for a long time without a plan, because a good line usually turns up somewhere in the middle. He's right about that, and a few of those lines are below.

The band he's toured with for the past year is younger than Halyard ever was. The drummer, Ines Okafor, is 26 and learned half the old catalog from concert videos before her audition. Rourke says she plays the songs faster than he wrote them, and that he's stopped asking her to slow down.

Low Tide Radio was recorded over eleven days in a converted boathouse outside Cork. Rourke wrote most of the lyrics in a notebook he kept in his coat pocket, and he admits that two of the songs started as letters he never sent. The producer, Marta Lindqvist, kept the vocal takes rough on purpose. She told me she wanted the record to sound like a person standing close to you.

He still won't say whether Halyard will play together again. When I ask, he laughs and changes the subject to football, then comes back to it a few minutes later without being prompted. He says the door isn't locked, and he also says nobody has knocked on it.

By the end of the call, the hotel bar downstairs is closing and someone is vacuuming the corridor outside his room.
